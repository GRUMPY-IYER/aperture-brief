GV Wire

OpenAI cuts Cursor off from its models after SpaceX buys it

OpenAI says it will stop serving its models to Cursor, citing contract compliance, days after SpaceX acquired the coding tool; Anthropic said it would expand Claude's availability inside Cursor to cover the gap. The Altman-Musk feud is the headline, but the mechanism is the point: a product's core capability was switched off by a supplier, not by a regulator.

Tech 101 for newbies

Today's Concept, Explained Simply
Model dependency (and the abstraction layer)

Most AI products do not own a model; they rent one over an API. If the supplier changes its terms, its price or its mind — as OpenAI just did with Cursor — the product's central feature can stop working without a line of your own code changing. The defence is an abstraction layer: a thin internal interface your app talks to, with adapters behind it for two or more providers, so switching is a config change rather than a rewrite. The cost is that you can only use features every provider supports.

Interconnect

Training a large model needs thousands of chips working on one problem, which means they must constantly exchange partial results. The wiring and switching that carries that traffic is the interconnect, and once a cluster is big enough it, not the chips, is the bottleneck — the processors sit idle waiting for data. This is why AI hardware is increasingly sold and compared by the rack rather than by the chip.

Philosophy 101 for newbies

Today's Concept, Explained Simply
The demarcation problem

How do you tell science from things that look like science? Karl Popper's answer was falsifiability — a real scientific claim tells you in advance what would prove it wrong. The trouble, which the Bigfoot piece illustrates, is that careful observation, instruments and record-keeping can all be present while the enterprise still fails to be science, and no single criterion has ever cleanly drawn the line.

The dichotomy of control

The Stoic division of the world into what is 'up to us' (our judgements, intentions and assent) and what is not (our body, reputation, the behaviour of others, outcomes). The practical claim is that suffering comes from treating the second category as if it were the first. The standard objection is that the line is drawn far too tightly — almost everything anyone cares about ends up on the far side of it.
