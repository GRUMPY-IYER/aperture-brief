Top Tech

TechCrunch

Is Alice Huang a Spy or Not?

A classified March memo reportedly named Alice Huang — the Megaspeed executive whose affair with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang briefly cost him his job — a Chinese intelligence asset, based on contact U.S. intelligence tracked between her and an officer of China's Ministry of State Security. Senior officials have since told Congress there's no evidence Beijing directed the relationship, and Megaspeed, already under scrutiny for allegedly funneling Nvidia chips to China, denies wrongdoing.

Why it matters for you: A reminder that personnel risk, geopolitics and export-control exposure can tangle together at the top of even the most valuable company in the world — vet key relationships as rigorously as you vet the supply chain.

404 Media

Google Wants to Make a 24/7 AI TV Channel. You'll Be the Main Character.

A newly surfaced Google patent describes an always-on, AI-generated TV channel that would build personalized "shows" and ads for each viewer out of data already collected about them. 404 Media frames the filing as an early marker of an "AI video endgame": infinite, hyper-targeted content generated on demand rather than programming anyone else watches.

Why it matters for you: A patent filing like this is an early signal of where a platform wants to take ad-supported content next — worth tracking before it ships as a feature you have to react to.

TechCrunch

X Finally Gets Parental Controls After 20 Years

X now defaults teen accounts (ages 13-17) to a "protected mode" that hides sensitive content and blocks targeted ads, and gives parents tools to cap daily time, approve followers and filter words — though not to read messages or see search history. The rollout follows months of criticism over X's AI chatbot generating sexualized images and the platform's long-running child-safety problems.

Why it matters for you: A trust-and-safety feature arriving two decades late shows how much regulatory and reputational pressure it now takes to move a platform that's resisted parental controls this long.

ScienceDaily

Astronomers May Have Discovered a Black Hole Born Before the First Stars

A brief gravitational-lensing flash in the Large Magellanic Cloud, nicknamed Phoebe, may be a primordial black hole roughly three times the Moon's mass — far too small to have formed from a collapsing star, and potentially 13 billion years old. Swinburne University researchers say it's about 100,000 times more likely to belong to the galaxy's dark matter halo than to ordinary matter, though a rogue planet remains a rival explanation.

ScienceDaily

Plastic Waste Becomes High-Performance Oil

Virginia Tech chemists converted PVC, one of the hardest plastics to recycle, into a synthetic lubricant base oil in about three hours, dissolving it with aluminum trichloride and alpha-olefins and heating the mix to 70°C. The team, with partners at Texas A&M and Caltech, says old pipes, window frames and credit cards could become feedstock for "green" engine oil, though scaling the process beyond the lab remains the next step.

ScienceDaily

Harvard's Tiny UV Light Chip Could Help Shrink Quantum Computers

Harvard engineers built a microscopic chip that converts red light into ultraviolet using a new "sidewall poling" technique on lithium niobate, producing 120 times more UV output than earlier thin-film devices. Trapped-ion quantum computers need bulky external UV lasers for many operations, so an on-chip source could make the machines significantly more compact.

Phys.org

Why Fertile Male Killifish Outlive Their Peers

A Leibniz Institute on Aging study of turquoise killifish found males with greater early-life reproductive success tended to live longer, the opposite of the usual assumption that reproduction trades off against lifespan. Researchers suggest both traits may instead reflect a shared underlying "quality" in the individual fish, though whether that holds in other species, or humans, remains open.

The Pretendian Wars

Dina Gilio-Whitaker argues that falsely claiming Indigenous identity is a grave, systemic harm rather than a victimless mistake, since it diverts jobs, grants and resources meant for actual Native people. She likens such claims to stolen valour and urges anyone repeating an unverified family story to research it, and stop telling it if the evidence doesn't hold up.

Aeon

A Colossal Oscillation

Historian Dagomar Degroot traces how El Niño has repeatedly driven droughts and famines, from ancient Peru to 19th-century India and Ethiopia, while a few groups profited — a pattern he attributes less to the weather itself than to conquest and inequality in how its harms are distributed. He argues El Niño's modern image as a predictable global catastrophe owes much to Cold War-era science and media, and that today's geopolitical rivalries could make its future impacts deadlier still.

Aeon

Catwalks and Crackdowns

Parisa Khoshnami writes that young women in Tehran are reshaping public life simply by dressing as they choose, an everyday "art of presence" that has quietly forced the state to loosen hijab enforcement even as most of the women insist their choices aren't political. She argues the government's own crackdowns on cafés and social accounts reveal just how political those choices really are.

Philosophy 101 for newbies

Today's Concept, Explained Simply
Pretendian

A term for someone who falsely claims Indigenous ancestry or identity, often to access jobs, grants or resources set aside for Native people — treated by critics like Gilio-Whitaker as a serious form of theft, not a harmless mistake.

Art of presence

Sociologist Asef Bayat's phrase for how ordinary people change what's politically possible just by repeating small, everyday acts in public — like Tehran women going without headscarves — until the state can no longer enforce a rule it never formally repealed.
