TechCrunch

A Decade-Long Grid Mystery Traced to Batteries Checking Their Charge on the Same Clock Tick

German researchers found that internet-synchronized uninterruptible power supplies and grid batteries all check their charge at the same moment each minute, creating a visible frequency wobble that has roughly quadrupled in Ireland since 2016 as data centers grew. The fix: manufacturers should randomize the timing instead of syncing to the clock.

Why it matters for you: This is a reminder that synchronized clocks are an externality: a design choice that's invisible and harmless at small scale becomes a systemic risk once enough devices adopt it, and nobody owns the fix until regulators or standards bodies step in.

Tech 101 for newbies

Today's Concept, Explained Simply
Key-person risk

The exposure a company carries when its value depends heavily on one individual's judgment or reputation — why a personal scandal involving Nvidia's CEO can move a $4.5 trillion valuation, independent of whether any secret actually leaked.

Synchronized demand spike

What happens when many independent devices are all set to act on the same clock tick — like batteries worldwide checking their charge at the top of every minute — turning an invisible design choice into a visible, shared-system disturbance.

Aeon

We Need a Word for Grief That Isn't About Death

Derek van Zoonen argues grief follows any loss that disrupts someone's identity, not just bereavement — an estranged parent, a faded friendship — yet Western culture and psychiatry recognize almost none of it, a gap he calls disenfranchised grief. Left unnamed, he writes, that "love with nowhere to go" tends to resurface later as physical or emotional symptoms instead.

Philosophy 101 for newbies

Today's Concept, Explained Simply
Disenfranchised grief

Grief that follows a real loss — an estrangement, a friendship's end — which culture and medicine don't officially recognize as grief, leaving people to carry it without ritual, language, or support.

Dual diagnosis

Psychiatry's old rule against diagnosing two overlapping conditions, like autism and ADHD, in the same person at once — dropped only in the DSM-5 in 2013, which is why naming the combined AuDHD experience is so recent.
