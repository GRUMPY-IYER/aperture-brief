404 Media Investigative

ICE Is Paying AT&T to Dig Through a Mountain of Call Records

An internal ICE document shows the agency is paying AT&T more than $2 million a year for a "Data Analytical Services" program that privacy advocates call Hemisphere surveillance by another name, letting investigators mine call records and location data via an administrative subpoena rather than a warrant. AT&T says the work is covered by valid legal demands; critics including Sen. Ron Wyden call it warrantless surveillance with no judicial oversight.

Why it matters for you: A case study in lock-in: once a sole-source vendor is the only one with a capability, a 'program' survives its own officially-announced shutdown — the same dynamic that makes migrating off an entrenched data vendor so hard inside a product org.

TechCrunch

UK Government Forced to Disclose Secret Apple Backdoor Order

The UK's Investigatory Powers Tribunal ruled the Home Office must confirm it secretly ordered Apple to build a way into customers' encrypted iCloud data, rejecting the government's national-security objection after rights group Liberty sued. Apple refused the order and instead disabled Advanced Data Protection for UK users rather than weaken its encryption everywhere, calling the ruling an overdue step toward transparency.

Why it matters for you: The sharpest live test yet of 'lawful access' versus end-to-end encryption: Apple's actual answer was to turn off a security feature for one country rather than weaken it globally — a tradeoff any PM shipping E2E encryption across multiple legal regimes should expect to face explicitly, not hypothetically.

Tech 101 for newbies

Today's Concept, Explained Simply
Administrative subpoena

A records request a government agency can issue on its own, without asking a judge. ICE's AT&T data program runs on these instead of warrants — exactly what privacy advocates object to, since no court ever reviews whether the request is justified.

Lawful access vs. end-to-end encryption

Governments sometimes demand a way to read encrypted data for investigations; companies that build genuine end-to-end encryption can't comply without weakening it for everyone. Apple's answer to the UK's order was to turn off the encryption feature for UK users rather than build the backdoor — a real-world resolution of a debate usually only discussed in the abstract.

ScienceDaily

Why Hydrogen Flames Can Glow Orange, Then Turn Nearly Invisible

University of Adelaide engineers tracked hydrogen flames for two weeks and found their visible glow depends almost entirely on windborne sodium, from sources like sea salt, rather than the fuel itself — the sodium signal varied 28-fold between readings. Because hydrogen flames are far dimmer than natural-gas ones and can vanish to the eye entirely, the team argues appliance safety should shift from "look for the flame" to UV, infrared or temperature-based detectors.

Mercury-Bound X-ray Telescope Delivers First Images After Eight Years in Space

ESA/JAXA's BepiColombo mission opened the eyes of its Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer for the first time, training it on the Crab Nebula across 39 hours to calibrate it ahead of Mercury orbit insertion on November 21. The detector shows zero radiation damage after eight years in transit, and once operational will map Mercury's surface composition — the first imaging X-ray telescope ever flown on a planetary mission.

The researchers say the same isolation dynamics likely apply to fragmented mainland habitats, making this a usable conservation framework beyond islands alone.

In Praise of the Dilettante

Philosopher Jay Miller argues the dilettante — the deeply engaged amateur who refuses to specialize — is a defensible, even admirable way to live against a culture that prizes single-minded mastery. Drawing on Kierkegaard's aesthete and Nietzsche's free spirit, he reframes dilettantism as a mode of abundance rather than a lack of seriousness, while conceding it rarely brings recognition or acclaim.

Aeon

Why Church-State Separation Rests on a Psychological Distinction, Not Just a Legal One

Philosopher Neil Van Leeuwen and legal scholar Jacob Eisler argue that church-state separation rests on a hidden psychological distinction: "religious credence" doesn't update with evidence and stays compartmentalized to sacred contexts, unlike ordinary factual belief. That difference, not religion's subject matter, is what justifies keeping the state out of establishing belief while still protecting free exercise.

Philosophy 101 for newbies

Today's Concept, Explained Simply
Religious credence

A way of holding an idea that doesn't update with evidence and stays confined to sacred moments and rituals — unlike ordinary factual belief, which responds to evidence and governs everyday action. The distinction, not religion's subject matter, is what philosophers argue justifies keeping church and state separate.

Positive and negative freedom

Negative freedom is room to move within a practice without obstruction; positive freedom is the active drive to explore and make your mark. The dilettante, who roams across pursuits without being tied to mastering one, draws on both to justify a life of breadth over specialization.
