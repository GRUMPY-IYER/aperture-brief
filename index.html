US Politics

OPB

Supreme Court Leaves Pause on Trump's Trans Prison Policy in Place

The Supreme Court declined Wednesday to lift a lower court's block on a Bureau of Prisons policy that would have ended hormone therapy for transgender federal inmates, offering no explanation in its unsigned order. Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito said they would have granted the administration's request; the underlying case, representing roughly 1,000 inmates with a gender dysphoria diagnosis, continues in the lower courts.

NOTUS

DOJ Says It's Close to Fielding 1,000 Election Monitors for the Midterms

Civil Rights Division chief Harmeet Dhillon said the Justice Department is close to fully staffing the 1,000 federal monitors it plans to send to polling places in November, telling Real America's Voice "we're pretty close to that number of people who've raised their hands." She has framed the monitors as observers checking on voting-access barriers and fraud rather than enforcers, even as DOJ has lost nearly two dozen of the more than 30 lawsuits it has filed seeking unredacted state voter rolls.

Al Jazeera

UN Experts Urge India to Stop 'Excessive Force' Against SIR Protesters

More than 20 independent UN human rights experts called on Indian authorities to immediately stop using excessive force against demonstrators protesting the Election Commission's voter-roll revision, citing lathi charges on unarmed students and more than 7,000 reported detentions in and around New Delhi. They also raised concern about reports of sexual violence in detention and urged an independent investigation; India's government rejected the statement as resting on unverified claims.

Hormuz Tanker Attacks Stay Elevated as Iran War Grinds On

A Kuwaiti-flagged tanker caught fire after an unknown projectile struck it off Oman early Wednesday, a day after another tanker was hit near Iraq's coast — crews were reported safe in both cases. Top Tech

Axios

AI Tools Helped Uncover a Critical Flaw Across Much of the Web's Backend

Security researchers used AI tools to uncover two critical PHP vulnerabilities, rated 9.2 out of 10 for severity, that could let an attacker take over a server with no login needed — flaws in a language that still runs the backend of roughly 70% of websites whose server language is known. Finland and the Netherlands took some government sites offline as a precaution and the NSA urged organizations to patch immediately; accounts differ on exactly which AI tools found the bugs first.

Why it matters for you: AI-assisted vulnerability research is now fast enough to find critical bugs before attackers do — but that same speed cuts both ways once adversaries adopt it too.

CVSS score

A 0-to-10 industry scale for how severe a software vulnerability is. The PHP flaws found this week scored 9.2, meaning an attacker could potentially take over a server without even logging in.

AI-assisted vulnerability research

Using AI models to scan code for exploitable bugs faster than a human team could alone — the same technique security researchers and criminals are both racing to use first.

Chemistry World

A Solvent-Free Way to Recycle Acrylic Glass Back Into Its Building Blocks

Chemistry World reports that Athina Anastasaki's group at ETH Zurich has found what they call the first chemical recycling route for acrylic glass (PMMA) that works below 200°C, far cooler than the 400°C-plus pyrolysis usually needed. The method needs no solvent and no light irradiation and works on ordinary commercial PMMA, making it a more realistic candidate for scaling up than earlier lab techniques that needed specially engineered plastic.

ScienceDaily

Scientists Redate a Famous 'Space Weather' Train Delay by Seven Years

A Lancaster University-led team has shown that a celebrated 1871 account of solar activity delaying an Exeter train by 16 minutes couldn't have happened in 1841 as long assumed, since the rail line didn't exist yet, and matched old timetables and Greenwich magnetic observatory records instead to October 1848. The correction, published in the journal Space Weather, fixes a 178-year-old error in one of the earliest known cases of a solar storm disrupting human infrastructure.
