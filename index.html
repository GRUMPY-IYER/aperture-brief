How to Assemble Science: Why Single Studies Keep Contradicting Each Other

Helen Pearson argues science's deepest flaw isn't bad studies but its failure to synthesise them — single results get reported in isolation, producing contradictory headlines. She makes the case that unglamorous work like meta-analysis deserves far more credit than the pursuit of novelty.

Aeon

What Three Centuries of 'Equality' Talk Reveal About the Word's Limits

Teresa Bejan traces how 'equality' has carried conflicting senses — balance, indifference, parity — since 17th-century Levellers used that ambiguity to demand commoners be treated as the aristocracy's peers. She argues equality becomes real only through shared practices, like jury service, not through the word alone.

Aeon

That Gut Feeling a Stranger Is 'Off'? Philosophers Say It Counts as Evidence

Marc Moffett argues everyday intuitions justify belief much like perception does, presenting their content as simply actual rather than needing further inference. That framing ties the hunches people act on daily to the same intuitions philosophers lean on in thought experiments.

Philosophy 101 for newbies

Today's Concept, Explained Simply
Intuition (as evidence)

A quick, non-inferential sense that something is true or wrong — not a feeling, philosophers increasingly argue, but a mode of 'just seeing' that can count as real evidence, the same way spotting a tree does.

Meta-analysis

A study of studies: pooling many smaller results on the same question to find the pattern that no single trial, however well done, can show on its own.
